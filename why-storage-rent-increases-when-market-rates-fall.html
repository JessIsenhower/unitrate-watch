Storage market rates are falling. So why did your rent go up?

Because self-storage does not have one price. Operators compete aggressively for new move-ins, then may raise rates for customers who stay. Industry filings make that pricing model unusually visible.

Short answer

A softer market can make new-customer deals cheaper without making your existing rent cheaper.

In July 2026, national self-storage advertised rents were 1.6% below a year earlier. Yet Yardi Matrix reported that self-storage REIT in-place rents had grown 0.5% in Q2. Those two facts are not contradictory. They describe two different parts of the pricing system.

Advertised or street rates are largely acquisition prices. They help fill empty units. In-place rents are what current customers are paying after they have already moved in, sometimes after one or more rate changes.

Sources: Yardi Matrix, August 2026 national report ↗ · Yardi Matrix, Q2 2026 REIT analysis ↗

The industry has a name for this: ECRI.

ECRI means Existing Customer Rate Increase. It is not a term invented by angry renters. It appears in self-storage investment research and operator discussions as part of revenue management.

Why ECRIs matter to the business model

Self-storage is usually rented month to month. Once a customer has moved furniture, boxes, inventory, or household belongings into a unit, switching has a real cost: time, labor, a truck, new fees, and the unpleasant prospect of touching every box again.

That creates a different economic decision for an existing customer than for a shopper comparing empty units online. Operators can use low move-in pricing to compete for a vacancy, then evaluate how much existing-customer pricing can rise without causing too many move-outs.

Global Self Storage said in its 2025 annual report that increasing existing-tenant rates is a key component of revenue growth and that it evaluates increases partly by the expected effect on incremental move-outs. It also warned that when long-term customers leave, replacing them with new customers at lower rates can cause rent "roll down."

Source: Global Self Storage 2025 Annual Report, SEC filing ↗

Public company filings show the gap in actual operating data.

Public Storage's Q2 2026 filing gives a useful window into the difference between customers arriving and customers leaving its same-store portfolio.

The move-out rate was about 43% higher than the move-in rate. The overall in-place contract rate was even higher.

That is powerful evidence of a pricing gap, but it needs a caveat: these are portfolio averages, not matched identical units. Unit size, geography, climate control, promotion mix, tenure, and customer mix can all differ. The numbers explain the system. They do not prove that a particular renter is being overcharged.

Public Storage also says it regularly adjusts rental rates and promotional discounts to maximize revenue from new customers, then increases rents for existing customers over time. It explicitly says long-term customers are an important factor in revenue growth.

Source: Public Storage Form 10-Q for quarter ended June 30, 2026 ↗

This is not unique to one storage company.

Extra Space Storage

Extra Space reported approximately 2.45 million tenants at the end of 2025, primarily on month-to-month leases. Its annual report says existing tenants generally receive rate increases at least annually. It also describes revenue-management systems that can analyze and adjust rental rates daily across the portfolio in response to market conditions.

Source: Extra Space Storage 2025 Form 10-K ↗

CubeSmart

CubeSmart's 2025 annual report says its self-storage agreements are typically month to month and, subject to state law, generally provide flexibility to increase rental rates over time as market conditions permit.

Source: CubeSmart 2025 Form 10-K ↗

Independent industry research

Inland's 2025 Self-Storage Sector Review described a roughly 43% spread between asking and in-place rents, compared with about 10% historically. The report specifically connected that spread to operators' ability to raise rents on existing tenants through ECRIs.

Source: Inland 2025 Self-Storage Sector Review ↗

So is your increase unfair?

The market data cannot answer that by itself, and neither can the size of the percentage increase.

A 40% increase can still leave a renter below comparable local rates. A 10% increase can leave someone far above the price of genuinely comparable units nearby. Promotional rates can also make a cheap-looking alternative temporary.

The useful test is more concrete.

  1. Calculate the new annual cost. A $60 monthly increase is $720 a year.
  2. Check your current facility first. Look for the same size and genuinely similar unit attributes.
  3. Separate promotions from durable pricing. A one-month discount is not a long-term comparison.
  4. Check nearby alternatives. Match climate control, floor, drive-up access, hours, fees, and material unit differences.
  5. Price the hassle of moving. Truck, labor, time, new admin charges, locks, and the risk of another introductory rate all count.
  6. Negotiate with evidence. Ask for the best existing-customer rate available today and show the comparable public pricing you found.
