Why this exists · August 2026

We tried to answer one simple question about storage rent. It turned into a much bigger one.

If your storage company raises your rent, how do you know whether the new price is still reasonable? We expected to find a straightforward answer. Instead, we found a market where the price offered to a new renter and the price paid by an existing renter can behave very differently.

Our conclusion

Self-storage has a price-transparency problem, not a simple “high prices” problem.

We do not think every storage rent increase is unfair. We do think renters should be able to see whether the rate they are being asked to pay is still competitive with genuinely comparable units nearby. That is surprisingly difficult to answer today.

We started with the obvious approach: look at market prices.

That sounds easy. Search the facility, find the same unit size, compare the current advertised price with the rent-increase notice, and decide.

But the deeper we looked, the less useful a single advertised number became. Storage pricing can include temporary promotions, different floors, climate control, drive-up access, fees, protection plans, and different rates for people moving in versus people who are already occupying a unit.

The useful question was no longer “Is there a cheaper listing?” It became: What is the durable price for a genuinely comparable unit, and is the difference large enough to justify negotiating or physically moving your belongings?

1

We found evidence that new-customer pricing and existing-customer pricing can move in opposite directions.

In July 2026, national advertised self-storage rents were 1.6% lower than a year earlier, according to Yardi Matrix. Yet industry reporting also showed in-place rents at major public operators still growing.

That is not necessarily contradictory. Advertised rates are largely used to attract new move-ins. Existing customers are already inside the facility and may receive later rate adjustments under their rental terms and applicable law.

2

We found that existing-customer increases are not an accidental side effect. They are part of revenue management.

The industry even has a common term for them: Existing Customer Rate Increases, or ECRIs.

Global Self Storage's 2025 annual report says increasing existing-tenant rental rates is a key component of revenue growth. It says the company evaluates the level of those increases partly by the expected effect on incremental move-outs. The same filing notes the risk of “roll down” when a long-term customer leaves and is replaced by a new customer at a lower rate.

Extra Space Storage's 2025 filing similarly describes month-to-month leases that allow rates to increase over time as market conditions permit. Its annual report describes a sophisticated revenue-management platform built around pricing, promotions, demand, and customer behavior.

None of that means a company is doing something improper. Revenue management exists in many industries. What caught our attention was the information imbalance: operators can model the economics of a renter staying or leaving with considerable sophistication, while the renter often sees only one number on a notice.

Sources: Global Self Storage 2025 Annual Report, SEC ↗ · Extra Space Storage 2025 Form 10-K, SEC ↗

3

Then we found a regulator had challenged exactly the kind of pricing behavior we were trying to understand.

In July 2026, New York City announced a $1.7 million settlement with Extra Space Storage after the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection brought an enforcement action alleging deceptive and exploitative practices.

The settlement included $1 million for consumer restitution, more than $700,000 in civil penalties, and compliance terms addressing several alleged practices. Among them, the city specifically described attracting customers with low prices and then raising rates excessively.

The city's restitution criteria now include some customers whose rent increased within the first six months or by more than 50% during the covered rental period, along with customers affected by certain property-condition or auction issues.

This did not prove that every Extra Space increase, or every self-storage increase generally, is deceptive. It did tell us the pricing question was substantial enough to have moved beyond internet complaints and into formal consumer-protection enforcement.

Sources: NYC settlement announcement ↗ · NYC Extra Space settlement claim criteria ↗

4

We noticed something else: price predictability is becoming a product feature.

In March 2026, U-Haul introduced a one-year storage price lock for new customers. Security Public Storage currently advertises no rate increases for the first six months, future increases capped at 10%, and a policy that existing customers will not pay more than new customers for the same standard unit rate after promotions.

Those are company marketing claims, not independent proof about the entire market. But they matter for a different reason. Operators are explicitly competing on price stability and loyalty pricing. That only works as a differentiator if surprise increases and incumbent-versus-new-customer pricing are things consumers care about.

5

The missing piece was not another list of cheap storage units.

We could find comparison sites, operator listings, reviews, legal explainers, and plenty of angry customer stories. What we did not find was a simple independent workflow built around the decision an existing renter is actually facing:

“My rent just went from X to Y. Is Y still competitive enough that I should stay, or is the gap large enough to negotiate or move?”

Answering that responsibly requires more than sorting listings from lowest to highest. It means checking the current facility first, separating promotions from durable pricing, matching material unit attributes, accounting for fees, and putting the savings next to the very real friction of moving.

That gap is what we decided to test with UnitRate Watch.

What we are building, and what we are not.

UnitRate Watch is not a rate-shaming site. A large increase is a reason to investigate, not proof that a company acted illegally or unfairly.

It is not a fake precision machine. Two 10x10 units can differ in ways that materially affect price. When the public data is incomplete or a promotion makes the comparison uncertain, we should say that.

It is not legal advice. Rental agreements, notice requirements, and state or local law determine what an operator is allowed to do.

And it should not become pay-to-rank. If UnitRate Watch eventually earns referral revenue, a payment should never determine which storage option is presented as the best value.

The product thesis

Give the renter the market context the rent-increase notice does not.

For now, the beta is deliberately simple: calculate the increase, request an anonymous public-market scan, and get a read on whether the evidence points toward staying, negotiating, or switching.

There is one more thing we want to learn.

We may be wrong about the size of the problem.

If our scans show that most existing-customer rates remain reasonably competitive after a rent increase, we should publish that. If customers do not care enough about the gap to check, negotiate, or switch, that matters too.

The longer-term opportunity is not to manufacture outrage. It is to build enough anonymous market evidence to answer a question that is currently hard for an individual renter to answer alone.

How much are existing storage customers actually paying relative to the market around them?

That is the question UnitRate Watch exists to investigate.

Research notes and limitations

  • Operator pricing pages describe those companies' own policies and marketing claims.
