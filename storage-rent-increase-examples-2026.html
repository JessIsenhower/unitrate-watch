How much are storage rents actually increasing?

There is no trustworthy single “average storage rent increase” that applies everywhere. But recent public examples show why renters should treat the move-in rate as a starting price, not automatically as a long-term price.

Important: these are examples, not an average.

This is a deliberately small set of publicly visible cases found on storage operators’ own webpages. It is not a representative sample, and it should not be used to claim that every renter will see the same increase. Reviews are self-reported. Unit attributes, promotions, local demand, notice terms, and fees can differ.

What these examples do show

They show that significant post-move-in changes can occur quickly enough that a renter’s decision should include price stability, not just the advertised move-in rate. They also show why negotiation can matter: several public operator responses direct renters to discuss the increase, and some reviews report that managers reduced or delayed increases.

They do not show that any particular increase is unlawful. Legality depends on the rental agreement, notice, and applicable state or local law.

What to compare before you move

A lower headline price is only useful if the alternative is truly comparable. Match unit size, climate control, floor/access, drive-up status, recurring fees, promotion duration, and the likely cost of moving. If you are shopping before renting, ask whether the operator offers a price lock or rate guarantee. U-Haul, for example, currently publishes a one-year base-rent price-lock program.
